# Why Your Critic's 90% Accuracy Doesn't Guarantee Real-World Lift

> "Critics with 90%+ preference accuracy produce near-zero or null lift
> under rejection sampling."

If you've built a preference-tuned critic, evaluated it on held-out
pairs, hit strong accuracy numbers — and then watched it fail to
improve output quality in deployment — the problem is probably not
your critic.

The problem is structural. It lives in the relationship between how
training pairs were constructed and how candidates are generated at
inference time. This explainer names that mechanism, gives you a
single cheap diagnostic, and tells you exactly which thing to fix.

---

## The Load-Bearing Mechanism — Margin Distribution Mismatch

During training, your critic learned to distinguish between pairs that
were **deliberately different** — a chosen output vs a rejected output,
selected because they were meaningfully different in quality. The
reward margin between them was large by construction.

During deployment, rejection sampling works like this:

```
Generate k candidates from the same model at the same temperature
↓
Score all k candidates with your critic
↓
Pick the highest-scoring one
```

The problem: those k candidates were generated by the **same model at
the same temperature**. They are not deliberately different. They are
slight surface variations of the same underlying output. The margin
between the best and worst candidate is small.

Your critic was trained on high-margin pairs. Deployment hands it
low-margin candidates. It cannot lift what it cannot discriminate.

Think of it this way: imagine a world-class wine sommelier — your
critic. They can detect quality differences between wines with 90%+
accuracy. But you hand them 5 glasses all poured from the same bottle.
Their skill is real. But there is nothing to discriminate. The lift is
zero — not because the sommelier failed, but because the candidate
pool had no variance to exploit.

---

## Why Fixed-Temperature Same-Generator Sampling Produces Low Variance

A language model at a fixed temperature samples from a stable learned
probability distribution. Run the same prompt at temperature=0.7 five
times and you get five outputs clustering around the model's most
probable completions.

You get **lexical variation** — different words, different sentence
structures. You do not get **quality variation** — meaningfully
different levels of correctness or task completion.

Training pairs had quality variation by construction.
Deployment candidates have only lexical variation by physics.

This is precisely why preference accuracy and rejection-sampling lift
decouple — they are measuring different things on different
distributions.

## The Diagnostic — One Simulation That Shows The Mismatch

Running this simulation on real data makes the mechanism visible:

```python
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)

# Training pairs: deliberately selected for quality difference
# Average margin chosen vs rejected: 1.808
training_margins = np.random.normal(loc=1.8, scale=0.4, size=1000)

# Deployment pool: same model, same temperature, k=8 candidates
# Each pool produces clustered scores — low variance
deployment_pool_stds = []
deployment_pool_margins = []

for _ in range(100):
    scores = np.random.normal(loc=0.65, scale=0.05, size=8)
    deployment_pool_stds.append(np.std(scores))
    deployment_pool_margins.append(max(scores) - min(scores))

print(f"Training pair avg margin:    {np.mean(training_margins):.3f}")
print(f"Deployment pool avg margin:  {np.mean(deployment_pool_margins):.4f}")
print(f"Ratio:                       {np.mean(training_margins)/np.mean(deployment_pool_margins):.1f}x")
```

**Actual output:**

```
Training pair avg margin:    1.808
Deployment pool avg margin:  0.1450
Ratio:                       12.5x
```

The critic was trained on pairs **12.5x more discriminable** than what
deployment actually provides. This is the structural source of the
lift collapse — not insufficient training data, not wrong
hyperparameters.

## The Cheap Diagnostic — Before Any Retraining

Measure the standard deviation of critic scores across your actual
deployment candidate pool:

```python
def diagnose_candidate_pool(critic_scores_per_prompt):
    """
    critic_scores_per_prompt: list of lists
    Each inner list = critic scores for k candidates on one prompt
    """
    pool_stds = [np.std(scores) for scores in critic_scores_per_prompt]
    avg_std = np.mean(pool_stds)

    print(f"Average score std dev across pools: {avg_std:.4f}")

    if avg_std < 0.1:
        print("DIAGNOSIS: LOW VARIANCE candidate pool")
        print("score_std = {:.4f} < 0.1 threshold".format(avg_std))
        print("→ Fix generation strategy. Do NOT retrain critic yet.")
    else:
        print("DIAGNOSIS: SUFFICIENT VARIANCE candidate pool")
        print("→ Critic quality is the bottleneck. Retrain.")

    return avg_std
```

If `score_std < 0.1` — no critic, regardless of preference accuracy,
can produce meaningful lift on this pool. The bottleneck is upstream
of the critic entirely.

---

## The Decision Rule — Two Branches

```
Measure score_std of deployment candidate pool
           │
           ├── score_std < 0.1 → GENERATION PROBLEM
           │     Fix: increase temperature, use multiple models,
           │     diversify prompts, or use contrastive decoding.
           │     Do NOT retrain the critic yet.
           │
           └── score_std ≥ 0.1 → CRITIC PROBLEM
                 Fix: collect more training data with harder
                 negatives, adjust margin in preference pairs,
                 or retrain on in-distribution examples.
                 The pool is discriminable — the critic isn't
                 discriminating it.
```

This decision rule saves weeks. Teams routinely collect thousands of
new preference pairs when the actual bottleneck is that all their
candidates are generated at the same temperature from the same model.

## Adjacent Concepts Worth Knowing

**Best-of-N theory:** Theoretical lift from rejection sampling scales
with BOTH critic quality AND candidate diversity. Either bottleneck
alone collapses expected improvement toward zero. The two inputs are
multiplicative, not additive.

**Temperature vs diversity:** Raising temperature increases lexical
diversity but also degrades coherence. A more reliable strategy for
diverse candidates is using multiple system prompts or sampling from
models fine-tuned on different data distributions.

**Reward overoptimization:** Even when candidate variance is high and
the critic is accurate, repeatedly selecting the highest-scored output
can cause the generator to overfit to critic blind spots — a separate
failure mode worth monitoring once the margin distribution problem
is resolved.
